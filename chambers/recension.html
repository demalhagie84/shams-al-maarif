The Living Recension

HOW THIS CORPUS STILL GROWS · A DOCUMENTED CASE, 2021–2026

The Book chamber says plainly that the Kubrā in circulation is not one author's book but a compilation that kept accreting around al-Būnī's authentic works for centuries after his death. That is nearly always said of the past — as though the process closed when the manuscript age closed. It did not. A compilation assembled in 2021 and revised in 2026 shows the same machinery still running, in English, inside living memory.

The work is Iḥyāʾ Uṣūl al-Ḥikmah — “The Revival of the Foundations of Wisdom” — compiled by Amin Patel of Ottawa, with a foreword by Syed Hamza Jalalpuri, and offered as khidma: given away rather than sold. A first edition appeared in March 2021; the second, running to some 2,401 pages, is dated August 2026. It is not a translation of one book. It is a recension — four Būnian and para-Būnian sources braided into a single run of twenty abwāb, with bracketed source references and folio citations left standing in the text.

Those visible seams are why it belongs in this edition. Most recensions hide their joins: the reader of a printed Kubrā cannot tell which sentence came from al-Būnī and which arrived four centuries later, and that undecidability is the central problem of the entire corpus. Here the joins are annotated. A compiler working now, with a stated method and a citation apparatus, has produced in five years a document of the same species as the one Cairo printed in 1345 AH — and watching him do it teaches us something about the copyists, printers, and unsigned hands whose additions this edition can otherwise only infer.

What this chamber claims — and what it does not

That a modern compilation exists is a fact about the twenty-first century, not evidence about the thirteenth. Nothing in Iḥyāʾ Uṣūl al-Ḥikmah tells us what al-Būnī wrote, and this edition cites it for exactly one purpose: as a datable, documented instance of the compiling process that produced the Kubrā. Its readings are not treated as authority for any Arabic text, its attributions are not adopted, and its khidma framing is reported rather than endorsed. The same taṣḥīḥ ethic that governs our account of the 1345 AH printing governs this one: describe the artifact, date it, name its compiler, and refuse to let recency pass for provenance.

Why the bulk of it is not reproduced here

The greater part of that recension is operative: chapters of compelling invocations, the Barhatiyya material, tried-and-tested awfāq and talismans, and a closing applied division treating possession and healing as procedure. This edition's Sealed Chamber policy applies to it without alteration and without regard to framing — no procedure, formula, timing, condition, or operative pairing from it appears anywhere on this site. Two further points of plain honesty. The compilation carries no licence, no publisher, and no grant of reproduction; its arrangement and English rendering remain its compiler's own even where the medieval sources beneath it are long in the public domain, and this chamber therefore describes and cites it rather than reproducing it. And the reason for sealing is the tradition's own, not a modern squeamishness: a book of workings was never meant to travel without a teacher, and a webpage is not a teacher.
