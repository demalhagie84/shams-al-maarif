A Comparative Study in Two Masters

The Laṭāʾif in Sulūk

اللطائف في السلوك

The subtle centres and their office on the path, as mapped by Aḥmad Sirhindī in the Maktūbāt and Shāh Walī Allāh al-Dihlawī in the Altāf al-Quds

Doctrine · Loci · Prophetic Feet · Colours · Ascent · Reconciliation

This codex is a full study of the laṭāʾif — the subtle centres of the wayfarer — in two of the great architects of Indian Sufism, with five illuminated plates set within the argument they illustrate. It maps the tenfold Mujaddidī scheme of Aḥmad Sirhindī as it lives in the Maktūbāt against the three-tiered expansion of Shāh Walī Allāh al-Dihlawī, chiefly from the Altāf al-Quds and the al-Tafhīmāt al-Ilāhiyya. Colour here is not ornament but argument: each centre is drawn as a shamsa, the illuminated sunburst of the manuscript tradition, and its fill encodes its rank.

How to read the plates — the colour of each laṭīfa

qalb yellow rūḥ red sirr white khafī black akhfā green

These are the five jewels of the ʿālam al-amr, shared by both shaykhs. Muted earthen tones mark the ʿālam al-khalq (the nafs and four elements); distinct jewel-tints (amethyst, indigo, silver-blue, gold-white, lilac) mark the centres Shāh Walī Allāh adds. A luminous rayed medallion is a subtle centre; a matte diamond is an element.

The Argument in Brief

  • Sirhindī systematised the classic Mujaddidī scheme of ten laṭāʾif — five of the ʿālam al-amr (qalb, rūḥ, sirr, khafī, akhfā) lodged in the chest, and five of the ʿālam al-khalq (nafs plus the four ʿanāṣir) — whose luminous awakening (tanwīr), performed by murāqaba and silent dhikr in ascending order, drives fanāʾ and constitutes the wayfarer's traverse of the two arcs (sayr ilā Allāh, fī Allāh, ʿan Allāh bi-Allāh, fī al-ashyāʾ). The laṭāʾif have origins (uṣūl) above the Throne, to which they return.
  • Shāh Walī Allāh inherited this Naqshbandī–Mujaddidī model but recast it into a three-tiered structure of some fifteen components, integrated with Avicennan–Galenic physiology (the nasama), adding the laṭīfa ʿaql at the middle level and five concealed centres (al-laṭāʾif al-kāmina) at the top — so that the two summits, prophetic inheritance and greater sainthood, become parallel rather than ranked: an architectural embodiment of his taṭbīq (reconciliation) of waḥdat al-wujūd and waḥdat al-shuhūd.
  • The two converge on the Command/creation cosmology and the ascent-through-purification logic, but diverge sharply in emphasis — Sirhindī toward struggle, descent, servanthood, and shuhūd; Walī Allāh toward cosmological symmetry, synthesis, and balance — and in structure: Sirhindī's practical fivefold/tenfold ladder against Walī Allāh's speculative three-tiered fifteenfold map.

Key Findings

Both figures inherit a common Kubrawī–Naqshbandī vocabulary of subtle centres but deploy it toward opposite intellectual temperaments. For Sirhindī the laṭāʾif are the practical machinery of a demanding contemplative ascent whose telos is annihilation and, crucially, the return to sober servanthood; for Shāh Walī Allāh they are primarily an explanatory cosmological-psychological schema through which he reconciles the warring metaphysical schools of Indian Sufism. Where Sirhindī's scheme is the operational curriculum of a living ṭarīqa — and remains so today — Walī Allāh's expanded fifteenfold model was a speculative refinement that, as Michael E. Asbury observes, “does not seem to have taken root,” since no lineages are found to adhere to it now.

A methodological caution runs through both cases. As ter Haar and Buehler establish, it is uncertain whether Sirhindī himself fixed the colours and precise bodily locations of the laṭāʾif; per Asbury, citing Buehler and Tosun & Bayraktar, “the first textual evidence for these appears in the writings of a later Mujaddidī, Mīr Muḥammad Nuʿmān” (d. ca. 1650). The famous colour scheme (qalb yellow, rūḥ red, sirr white, khafī black, akhfā green) and the prophetic correspondences are thus best described as Mujaddidī rather than strictly Sirhindian, though they crystallised around his letters.

I
The First Master

Sirhindī on the Laṭāʾif

اللطائف عند الإمام الرباني

Sirhindī's teaching on the subtle centres is dispersed across the letters, not set out in a single treatise; to read it well is to reconstruct a working curriculum from a master's correspondence with his disciples.

Sources and status

Sirhindī's doctrine is dispersed across the Maktūbāt-i Imām-i Rabbānī — three volumes (daftars) completed by their chronograms in 1025/1616–17, 1028/1618–19, and 1031/1621–22, containing 313, 99, and 122 letters respectively (534 letters to nearly 200 addressees). Friedmann established that the letters are arranged chronologically and that this ordering is Sirhindī's own, allowing us to trace doctrinal development; the laṭāʾif also figure in his earlier treatise Mabdaʾ o Maʿād. Arthur Buehler's Revealed Grace: The Juristic Sufism of Ahmad Sirhindi (Fons Vitae, 2011) provides full English translations of twenty-six letters with analysis; Yohanan Friedmann's Shaykh Ahmad Sirhindi (McGill, 1966; publ. 1971) and J.G.J. ter Haar's Follower and Heir of the Prophet (1992) are the standard monographic studies.

The cosmological frame

According to Sirhindī, God created the universe in stages: first the ʿālam al-amr (the World of Command), which came into being instantly at the divine “Kun!” (“Be!”); then the ʿālam al-khalq (the World of Creation), which unfolded through a gradual, quasi-evolutionary process; and finally the human being, in whom both worlds are combined, making the human a microcosm (ʿālam-i ṣaghīr) mirroring the macrocosm (ʿālam-i kabīr). God deposited within the human being ten laṭāʾif “as a trust” (amāna). Five belong to the ʿālam al-amr — qalb (heart), rūḥ (spirit), sirr (secret), khafī (hidden), and akhfā (most hidden); five belong to the ʿālam al-khalq — the nafs together with the ʿanāṣir arbaʿa (four elements): earth, water, fire, and air, which constitute the physical frame (qālib) and are collectively termed the laṭīfa qālibiyya. The Qurʾānic warrant for the amr/khalq distinction is 17:85, “the Spirit is from the command of my Lord” (al-rūḥ min amr rabbī), taken to mean the rūḥ originates in the immaterial, timeless realm of divine command.

Physical loci

In the Mujaddidī scheme the five laṭāʾif of the ʿālam al-amr are located in the chest: the qalb below the left breast; the rūḥ below the right breast; the sirr above the left breast (above the qalb); the khafī above the right breast (above the rūḥ); and the akhfā in the centre of the chest, between the heart and spirit. The nafs is located at the forehead (in some traditions below the navel), and the qālib/elements are distributed through the body. Sirhindī describes God placing the laṭāʾif of the ʿālam al-amr in the chest and “causing them to fall in love with” the body — the ʿArsh-i Muʿallā serving, in the cosmological telling, as the mediator between the ʿālam al-amr and the ʿālam al-khalq.

Prophetic correspondences — أقدام الأنبياء

Each laṭīfa receives its spiritual energy (fayḍ) from its corresponding cosmic origin under the “foot” (qadam) of a particular law-giving prophet, drawing on the doctrine — already in Ibn ʿArabī, who reports his vision that the awliyāʾ walk ʿalā aqdām al-anbiyāʾ, “in the footsteps of the prophets” — that the saints inherit prophetic footings. The standard Mujaddidī assignments are: qalb under the qadam of Ādam; rūḥ under Nūḥ and Ibrāhīm; sirr under Mūsā; khafī under ʿĪsā; akhfā under Muḥammad. Sirhindī's own controversial claim that his wilāya derived from the joint wilāya of Ibrāhīm and Mūsā reflects this schema of prophetic footings.

Colours

The Mujaddidī colour scheme is: qalb — yellow; rūḥ — red; sirr — white; khafī — black; akhfā — green. As noted above, the earliest textual attestation of this colour-and-location scheme is in Mīr Muḥammad Nuʿmān rather than demonstrably in Sirhindī's own hand — an attribution to keep in view whenever the plate below is read as “Sirhindian.”

Role in sulūk; the sequence of ascent

The laṭāʾif in their body-bound state are dimmed; the goal is to reverse this and restore their original luminous state — to awaken, illumine, or enlighten (tanwīr) them, which is to return them to their true form in their origins (uṣūl) in the ʿālam al-amr above the Throne (ʿarsh). This return is a necessary condition for fanāʾ in God. The shaykh guides the sālik to illumine the laṭāʾif one by one in ascending order, beginning with the qalb, chiefly through murāqaba (meditation) and silent dhikr (dhikr khafī, which the Naqshbandiyya privileges as the path of ʿazīma over the “licence” of vocal dhikr), aided by tawajjuh (the shaykh's directed spiritual attention) and rābiṭa (heart-bond with the shaykh). Each laṭīfa is assigned a divine name or Qurʾānic formula for dhikr. A hallmark of the method, inherited from Kubrawī precedent, is indirāj al-nihāyāt fī l-bidāyāt — “the incorporation of the endpoints into the beginnings”: beginning with the ʿālam al-amr and the heart rather than with the arduous prior purification of the nafs, and privileging jadhba (divine attraction) alongside sulūk. Sirhindī stresses that when the five laṭāʾif of the ʿālam al-amr are completed (mukammal), those of the ʿālam al-khalq are perfected automatically, and with them the “circle of contingency” (dāʾira-yi imkān).

The stages of sayr

The Naqshbandī–Mujaddidī journey is articulated as four (or five) journeys: sayr ilā Allāh (journeying to God — the vertical ascent from lower to higher stations until the states dissolve into the “Necessary Knowledge,” ʿilm al-wājib, culminating in fanāʾ); sayr fī Allāh (journeying in God — moving through the Names and Attributes to an indescribable state, identified with baqāʾ); sayr ʿan Allāh bi-Allāh (journeying from God by God — the descent back to the world of cause and effect); and sayr fī al-ashyāʾ / sayr min Allāh (journeying among things — movement within creation for the guidance of others). Sirhindī distinctively emphasises the descent (nuzūl) — the return to the world and the realisation of utter servanthood (ʿubūdiyya) — over the ascent, an inversion that gives his mysticism its sober, socially engaged, sharīʿa-centred character.

Fanāʾ and baqāʾ

The illumination of the laṭāʾif and the traverse of the circles map directly onto fanāʾ and baqāʾ. In the Mujaddidī cartography the sālik first seeks fanāʾ fī l-shaykh (revealing the dāʾira-yi imkān), then must break beyond the ʿālam al-amr via fanāʾ fī l-rasūl, then attains fanāʾ fī-Llāh. Completing the purification of the laṭāʾif beyond the fourth sphere yields baqāʾ bi-Llāh and the attainment of the nafs al-muṭmaʾinna (the soul at peace). For Sirhindī, crucially, witnessing (shuhūd), bewilderment (ḥayra), and gnosis (maʿrifa) all take place within the nafs (dar khwud); complete, unbounded (muṭlaq) fanāʾ requires that the outward gaze be entirely cut off. This is the epistemic core of his waḥdat al-shuhūd (unity of witnessing) as against Ibn ʿArabī's ontological waḥdat al-wujūd (unity of being), which he regarded as a lower, preliminary station. Buehler's twenty-first-century reassessment cautions that this “controversy” has been overstated: Sirhindī freely used Ibn ʿArabī's vocabulary, and the broad Sufi consensus treats the two as complementary vantage points rather than opposed doctrines.

Wilāya, maqāmāt, and tanwīr

Completing the five laṭāʾif of the ʿālam al-amr corresponds to traversing the Circle of Lesser Sainthood (dāʾira-yi wilāya-yi ṣughrā, the “shadow of the attributes,” associated with the awliyāʾ), while breaking beyond into the divine attributes yields the Circle of Greater Sainthood (wilāya-yi kubrā). Sirhindī subordinates the “path of sainthood” (ṭarīqa-yi awliyāʾ, followed by Bisṭāmī, Ḥallāj, Ibn ʿArabī) to the higher “path of prophecy” (ṭarīqa-yi anbiyāʾ), and links the highest stations to the “station of the call” (maqām-i daʿwa) proper to prophets. He claimed for himself the rank of qayyūm and the role of ṣila (connection) between the two oceans of exoteric and esoteric knowledge.

Distinctive contributions

Against the Kubrawī tendency — ʿAlāʾ al-Dawla Simnānī (d. 1336) had elaborated a laṭīfa subtler than the khafī, al-laṭīfa al-ḥaqqiyya, in a sevenfold model comprising qālab, nafs, qalb, sirr, rūḥ, khafī and ḥaqq, thus setting a precedent for the later introduction of the akhfā — Sirhindī returned to a standardised experiential five (plus the elemental five), and reoriented the whole system by insisting on the amr/khalq origins above the Throne and by making the descent and the reaffirmation of the sharīʿa the summit of the path. He was the first, per ter Haar, to fix the exact tenfold model that became standard for later Mujaddidīs; he refined the Kubrawī concept of indirāj al-nihāyāt fī l-bidāyāt; and he elaborated an unusually high prophetology in which the common believer can strengthen and share in the prophetic mission. His initially provocative ranking of the “reality of the Kaʿba” (ḥaqīqat-i kaʿba-yi rabbānī) and “reality of the Qurʾān” (ḥaqīqat-i qurʾān) above the “Muḥammadan reality” (ḥaqīqat-i muḥammadiyya) belongs to the same hierarchical mapping of realities above the laṭāʾif; by the late nineteenth century the Naqshbandī community had reversed this, placing the prophetic realities closer to God than the divine realities.

II
The Second Master

Shāh Walī Allāh on the Laṭāʾif

اللطائف عند شاه ولي الله الدهلوي

Walī Allāh takes the inherited five and rebuilds them into a cosmological architecture of his own — a psychology grounded in medicine and a metaphysics designed to reconcile the schools that divided the Sufis of his age.

Sources

Shāh Walī Allāh (1703–1762), initiated by his father Shāh ʿAbd al-Raḥīm into the Naqshbandiyya branch of Khwāja Khwurd (son of Sirhindī's own shaykh Bāqī bi-Llāh) as well as the Qādiriyya and Chishtiyya — and later the Shādhiliyya and Shaṭṭāriyya in the Ḥijāz — treated the laṭāʾif most fully in Altāf al-Quds fī Maʿrifat Laṭāʾif al-Nafs (translated by G.H. Jalbani, ed. D. Pendlebury, as The Sacred Knowledge of the Higher Functions of the Mind, 1982), with major developments in al-Tafhīmāt al-Ilāhiyya, and allusions in the Ḥujjat Allāh al-Bāligha (translated by Marcia Hermansen as The Conclusive Argument from God, Brill, 1996), the Hamaʿāt, Saṭaʿāt, Lamaḥāt, al-Budūr al-Bāzigha, and al-Qawl al-Jamīl. The authoritative studies are Marcia K. Hermansen's two articles — “Shāh Walī Allāh's Theory of the Subtle Spiritual Centers (Laṭāʾif),” Journal of Near Eastern Studies 47 (1988), and “Shāh Walī Allāh's Arrangement of the Subtle Spiritual Centers” (1982) — and J.M.S. Baljon's Religion and Thought of Shāh Walī Allāh Dihlawī (Brill, 1986), with historical context in S.A.A. Rizvi's Shāh Walī Allāh and His Times (1980).

Terminology and physiology

Walī Allāh grounds the laṭāʾif in Avicennan–Galenic (yūnānī) physiology — he had studied Ibn al-Nafīs's Mūjiz al-Qānūn, an abridgement of Ibn Sīnā's Qānūn. Food is digested into the four humours (akhlāṭ) in the liver; the subtlest portion becomes the “spirits” (arwāḥ), subtle vapours that bear the person's faculties. He uses nasama to name the spirit formed from the most subtle humours — the pneuma. The nasama that rises to the brain divides into the five outer senses and five inner senses (ḥiss mushtarak, takhayyul, tawahhum, ḥifẓ, idrāk); other portions become the “natural spirit” (al-rūḥ al-ṭabīʿī, in the liver) and the “animal spirit” (al-rūḥ al-ḥayawānī, in the heart). The laṭāʾif are not identical with the physical organs but are “local manifestations” of identically named parts of a higher cosmological realm above created things; they pervade the whole body. Hermansen notes the suggestive parallelism between the fivefold laṭāʾif and the five inner and five outer senses of yūnānī theory.

The three-tiered model

Where the Mujaddidīs had two levels — five immaterial laṭāʾif of the amr; nafs plus four elements of the khalq — Walī Allāh built a three-tiered model of some fifteen components. He did so by (a) adding the laṭīfa ʿaql (Intelligence) at the middle level, and (b) raising the khafī and adding an entirely new topmost tier of five “concealed” centres (al-laṭāʾif al-kāmina). Correspondingly he posits the spirit operating at three levels — the Pneuma (nasama) or airy spirit (rūḥ hawāʾī), which arises from the vapours of the elements and flows through the body “like fire in a coal or perfume in a rose”; the Rational Soul (al-nafs al-nāṭiqa), which bestows individual form (ṣūra nawʿiyya) and is “like a small aperture from the Realm of the Holy”; and the Heavenly Spirit (al-rūḥ al-samāwī), created long before the body and tied to the pre-existing form in the World of Images (ʿālam al-mithāl) and to qaḍāʾ and qadar. These map onto the three tiers: the Lower Soul relates most strongly to the Pneuma, the Intelligence to the Rational Soul, and the Heart to the Heavenly Spirit. He may have drawn the tripartite structure from al-Hujwīrī (rūḥ/nafs/jasad), from Ishrāqī and Neoplatonic precedent, and from Mullā Ṣadrā.

The middle circle and the “five jewels”

The standard Mujaddidī five are the “five jewels” (al-jawāhir al-khams): qalb, rūḥ, sirr, khafī, akhfā. In Walī Allāh's rearranged middle circle — the arena of voluntary self-transformation, in tension between angelic pull from above and animal pull from below — the Intelligence (ʿaql) sits on the left with the Mystery (sirr) above it (sirr being the higher refinement of ʿaql), and the Heart (qalb) sits on the right with the Spirit (rūḥ) above it (rūḥ being the higher refinement of qalb). The Rational Soul tops this circle; the Lower Soul (nafs) / Mould (qālab) anchors its bottom. The lowest circle contains nafs and the four elements. In a departure from Sirhindī, Walī Allāh reworked the element–laṭīfa correspondences to preserve symmetry across all tiers: in his letters he associates the Heart with earth, the Spirit with water (“water of life,” āb-i ḥayāt), and the Mystery with fire (“fire of Sinai,” ātish-i Ṭūr, cf. Qurʾān 28:29) — deliberately differing from the Sirhindī shaykhs, who associated rūḥ with air, sirr with water, khafī with fire, and akhfā with earth, leaving no element for the Heart.

The five concealed laṭāʾif — اللطائف الكامنة

Walī Allāh's innovative highest tier, diagrammed in al-Tafhīmāt al-Ilāhiyya (vol. 1, p. 244), comprises five centres below the Divine Essence (al-Dhāt): the akhfā (the Super-Arcane); the khafī (the Arcane); al-anāniyya al-kubrā (the Greater Selfhood); nūr al-quds (the Light of the Holy); and al-ḥajar al-baht / ḥajar-i baht (rendered “the Philosophers' Stone,” literally the “perplexing stone,” a term he takes explicitly from Ibn ʿArabī, especially the al-Tadbīrāt al-Ilāhiyya). Their functions, per Walī Allāh: the khafī is a “covering” over the Greater Selfhood and effects the realisation (maʿrifa) of the One Reality flowing through all existents; the anāniyya al-kubrā perceives its own qayyūmiyya (self-subsistence) throughout the universe — “an angel in the angel, a stone in the stone, a tree in the tree”; nūr al-quds knows “the arguing of the Highest Host” (al-malaʾ al-aʿlā) and the descending decree; and ḥajar-i baht fades, “without our knowing how” (bi-lā kayfa), into the Greatest Theophany (al-tajallī al-aʿẓam), so that the person's nature becomes “one of the limbs of the Truth” and the lower soul superfluous “like an extra finger.” Hermansen stresses that these represent a conceptual rather than a practical formulation.

The two summits and the position of akhfā

The topmost circle is divided into a right side — the Path of Prophetic Inheritance (wirātha al-nubuwwa), containing ḥajar-i baht and nūr al-quds, the locus of descent of the Greatest Theophany and of God's decrees (qadar) through the malaʾ al-aʿlā, and yielding annihilation (fanāʾ) in the Holy Spirit (al-rūḥ al-quds) — and a left side, the Path of the Greater Sainthood (al-wilāya al-kubrā), containing al-anāniyya al-kubrā and khafī, the path of ascent yielding annihilation in the Universal Soul (al-nafs al-kulliyya). The akhfā (Super-Arcane) sits at the foot of both paths: it is called “Super-Arcane,” Walī Allāh explains, precisely because the comprehensive knowledge obtained from following both courses is achieved only after both are completed — “the traveller on the mystic path arrives only after completing both of them.” This is Walī Allāh's structural masterstroke: prophecy and sainthood are no longer ranked one above the other, as in the Sirhindī-versus-wujūdī dispute over whether nubuwwa or wilāya is higher, but placed as parallel courses both of which the complete sālik must traverse; their integration is embodied in the “Inspired One” (muḥaddath / mufahham) at the pinnacle.

Role in sulūk; sequence; connection to metaphysics

At the initial stages the task is harmonisation (iʿtidāl) of the three lower centres — Heart (qalb), Intelligence (ʿaql), and Lower Soul (nafs), corresponding to heart, brain, and liver, and to emotion, rationality, and physical desire — so that the temperament (mizāj) becomes balanced; this is required of everyone for health, virtue, and salvation, and is precisely what the divine law (sharīʿa) exists to effect. The higher laṭāʾif (Spirit, Mystery, and the concealed centres) are not obligatory for all and require additional practices: the rūḥ is purified by constant purity, Qurʾān recitation, mystical exercises, and cultivating an intuitive relationship with the souls of the saints; the sirr is awakened by contemplating the divine attributes and Names and by silent, wordless dhikr. The whole ascent expresses Walī Allāh's master principle: at every level, internal conflict among ordered parts must be resolved into balance, whereupon the form expands to a higher order — driven by the universe's one great purpose, the all-comprehensive benefit (maṣlaḥa kulliyya). This is the metaphysics of his taṭbīq: his laṭāʾif model is, in Hermansen's phrase, simultaneously one of a conflict of forces and of one overriding great force, reconciling Sirhindī's conflict-laden waḥdat al-shuhūd with the monistic waḥdat al-wujūd of Ibn ʿArabī (a reconciliation set out at length in al-Tafhīmāt, vol. 2). Concepts such as tajallī (theophany), the ḥaẓīrat al-quds (the Realm of the Holy, from which the Rational Soul receives “like a small aperture”), the malaʾ al-aʿlā, and the ʿālam al-mithāl are woven directly into the functions of the higher centres.

III
The Two Compared

Convergence & Divergence

الاتفاق والاختلاف

Set against one another, the two maps share a common cosmological grammar and part decisively over temperament, structure, and the ranking of prophecy and sainthood.

Convergence

Both accept the amr/khalq cosmology and root the five higher laṭāʾif in the World of Command, with Qurʾān 17:85 as warrant. Both retain the core five jewels (qalb, rūḥ, sirr, khafī, akhfā) and the nafs-plus-elements substructure. Both understand sulūk as a graded ascent through purification and harmonisation toward fanāʾ and baqāʾ, requiring a shaykh, murāqaba, and dhikr. Both stand in the Naqshbandī silsila — Walī Allāh through the very line descending from Sirhindī's shaykh Bāqī bi-Llāh — and share the language of tanwīr, nisbat, and the “return.” Both, in their own registers, seek to reconcile Sufi experience with the sharīʿa; both were hailed as mujaddid, renewers of the faith.

Divergence

Their differences are equally fundamental. In structure, Sirhindī's is a two-level, five/tenfold, primarily practical ladder; Walī Allāh's is a three-tiered, fifteenfold, primarily speculative-cosmological map, adding the laṭīfa ʿaql and the five al-laṭāʾif al-kāmina that have no distinct top tier in Sirhindī. In physiology, Walī Allāh fuses the laṭāʾif with yūnānī medicine and the nasama, where Sirhindī keeps them more purely metaphysical. In direction and temperament, Sirhindī privileges descent, servanthood, and sober waḥdat al-shuhūd, insisting the outward gaze be severed; Walī Allāh privileges symmetry and synthesis, treating conflict as a stage to be harmonised within an overarching monistic purpose. On prophecy versus sainthood, Sirhindī ranks prophecy above sainthood, while Walī Allāh parallelises them as the two summit-paths meeting at the akhfā. On elemental correspondences they explicitly disagree (Heart–earth / Spirit–water / Mystery–fire in Walī Allāh versus rūḥ–air / sirr–water / khafī–fire / akhfā–earth in the Sirhindī shaykhs). And in reception, Sirhindī's model became the standard operative curriculum of the Mujaddidiyya worldwide, whereas Walī Allāh's fifteenfold expansion, as Asbury observes, “does not seem to have taken root,” being adopted by no known lineage.

The ascent sequence, side by side

For Sirhindī, the wayfarer illumines qalb → rūḥ → sirr → khafī → akhfā (the five of the ʿālam al-amr, in ascending order, in the chest), whereupon the ʿālam al-khalq laṭāʾif are perfected automatically; he then traverses fanāʾ fī l-shaykh → fanāʾ fī l-rasūl → fanāʾ fī-Llāh, attaining wilāya ṣughrā then kubrā, baqāʾ bi-Llāh, and the nafs muṭmaʾinna; and then descends through sayr ʿan Allāh bi-Allāh and sayr fī al-ashyāʾ back to servanthood and the guidance of others. For Shāh Walī Allāh, the sālik first harmonises the three lower centres (nafs, qalb, ʿaql) into a balanced mizāj — the work of the sharīʿa, required of all — then refines the higher manifest laṭāʾif (Heart→Spirit on the right, Intelligence→Mystery on the left) by specific practices; then ascends the two parallel summit-paths (wirātha al-nubuwwa via ḥajar-i baht and nūr al-quds → fanāʾ in al-rūḥ al-quds; and al-wilāya al-kubrā via al-anāniyya al-kubrā and khafī → fanāʾ in al-nafs al-kulliyya), both meeting at the Super-Arcane (akhfā) beneath the Divine Essence — the comprehensive knowledge attained only when both courses are complete.

For Further Engagement

Primary-text engagement. Read the laṭāʾif material in the Maktūbāt alongside Buehler's Revealed Grace (which translates the key letters and flags volume/letter numbers), and for Walī Allāh read Altāf al-Quds (Jalbani) together with the diagram in al-Tafhīmāt al-Ilāhiyya (vol. 1, p. 244) as decoded by Hermansen. Treat Hermansen's two articles as indispensable, and Friedmann and ter Haar as the anchors for Sirhindī.

Verify the colour/location attributions. Do not attribute the colour-and-location scheme directly to Sirhindī. Cite Mīr Muḥammad Nuʿmān (d. ca. 1650) via Buehler and Asbury as the earliest textual source, and present the colours as a Mujaddidī crystallisation.

Thresholds that would change the assessment. If a securely dated autograph or early recension of the Maktūbāt (or of Mabdaʾ o Maʿād) were shown to contain the explicit colour/location assignments in Sirhindī's own words, the “Nuʿmān-first” caveat should be dropped. Likewise, discovery of any lineage actually practising Walī Allāh's fifteenfold model would revise the “did not take root” verdict.

For comparative depth. Pursue the Kubrawī background — Najm al-Dīn Kubrā, Najm al-Dīn Rāzī's Mirṣād al-ʿibād (which grounds sirr and khafī in Qurʾān 20:7), and especially Simnānī's sevenfold prophet-colour scheme (transmitted to the Naqshbandiyya via Muḥammad Pārsā) — as the shared substrate against which both Indian figures should be read.

Colophon & Caveats

Principal sources. On Sirhindī: Yohanan Friedmann, Shaykh Ahmad Sirhindi; J.G.J. ter Haar, Follower and Heir of the Prophet; Arthur Buehler, Revealed Grace and Sufi Heirs of the Prophet. On Shāh Walī Allāh: Marcia K. Hermansen, “Shāh Walī Allāh's Theory of the Subtle Spiritual Centers” (JNES 47, 1988) and her translation of the Ḥujjat Allāh al-Bāligha; J.M.S. Baljon, Religion and Thought of Shāh Walī Allāh; S.A.A. Rizvi, Shāh Walī Allāh and His Times; and M.E. Asbury (Religions 13:690, 2022).

On the colours and loci. The exact colour-and-location scheme cannot be traced securely to Sirhindī's own pen; its earliest textual witness is Mīr Muḥammad Nuʿmān (d. ca. 1650). Treat it as a Mujaddidī crystallisation, not a personal signature. There is as yet no critical edition of the Maktūbāt, and Friedmann cautions that apparent contradictions among letters may reflect Sirhindī's deliberate withholding of “secrets” from unprepared disciples.

On Simnānī's colours. The seven-prophet colour list associated with the Kubrawī Simnānī is his own scheme, distinct from the Mujaddidī five and from Walī Allāh's assignments; it is referenced here as background but not depicted in the plates.

On the diagrams. Plates III–IV render Walī Allāh's arrangement as decoded by Hermansen from the diagram in al-Tafhīmāt al-Ilāhiyya (vol. 1). These plates are aids to understanding, not facsimiles; where precision matters, corroborate against Buehler, ter Haar, Friedmann, Hermansen, and Baljon.
